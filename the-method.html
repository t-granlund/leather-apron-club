I · THE PUPPYWhat Code Puppy was

Code Puppy began as an open-source project by Michael Pfaffenberger: a command-line apprentice that could read a codebase, write code, run its own checks, and fix what it broke — and it explained itself the whole way.

It was not magic and it was not a product you bought. It was a working animal: eager, a little opinionated, endlessly patient. You gave it a task in plain sentences. It thought out loud — you could watch its reasoning stream past, correct it mid-sentence, and steer it like dictating to a secretary who was already typing. If it died mid-job, it came back and picked up where it left off.

The whole temperament fit in four behaviors:

It does thisWhat that means for a person
It takes dictationYou speak the task on a drive. No new language to learn first.
It shows its workNothing happens in the dark. You watch it think, so you can trust it — or stop it.
You steer mid-runStart typing while it works and it changes course. The human is the master of the shop.
It survives itselfIt keeps its notes between visits. Progress is never lost to a crash.

Everything that follows — the seventy-five thousand, the director title, this club — grew out of those four behaviors and one decision: the tool adapts to the person's existing knowledge, never the reverse.

II · HOW IT WORKSThe four habits of a good master

Before the scale, there was the craft of working with the apprentice. It fits on an index card, and it is the actual training:

HabitWhy
Point before you speakStart it in the folder you want worked on. It cannot wander outside that drawer. Everything else stays untouched.
Ask for drafts, not miracles“Draft a reply” beats “handle my email.” Small, clear tasks come back right the first time.
Read before you trustIt shows its work. Give it a glance. It is diligent, but you are the master of the shop.
Steer mid-courseType while it works and it changes direction — like dictating to a secretary who is already typing.

That is the entire manual. A person who can write a sentence can run it on day one. That sentence is the whole onboarding.

IV · THE TRANSCENDENCEThe person who proved it

credentials optional

Here is the part that matters most, and it is a person, not a number.

My wife, Ashley, is thirty-five years old this month. She has no college degree. She is now Director of Agentic Work for all of Marketplace — and she is already transcending that.

She did not climb the ladder that was given. She built a new one, out of the thing this whole account is about: she took the tools to people who had been told the tools were not for them, and she organized the work the honest way — real requirements, clear timelines, and a standing refusal to pretend that any of this requires a credential to touch.

The tool never asked her where she went to school. It asked her what needed to be done. It turns out that was the only question that mattered.

Giving people the tools they need, with their existing knowledge, is all you need. Add honesty through requirements and clear timelines, and a non-graduate at thirty-four becomes the director of the thing at thirty-five.

V · THE METHODSix rules, distilled from all of it

This is the end-to-end method — the one you can run with your own council, your own team, your own town:

Start with what they already know.Existing knowledge is the foundation, never the obstacle. The tool meets the plumber in plumbing, the accountant in ledgers.
Speak the task.No new language before the first win. The plain sentence is the interface.
Honesty through requirements.Say what you actually need — not what sounds impressive. The tool, and the person, do better with the truth.
Clear timelines, small and dated.Trust is built by finished things. One task, this week, done — beats a roadmap for a someday.
Demonstrate, don't mandate.Nobody was ever trained by a memo. Do the work in front of them, on their work.
Every person trained becomes a trainer.The index card travels. This is how ten becomes seventy-five thousand — and how a town becomes a council.

VI · THE EXERCISESHow to run it yourself

Exercise one · the first evening

The one-task pledge.

Bring one task you would love to never do again — a pile of receipts, a messy folder, a letter you keep meaning to write. Speak it to the apprentice. Finish it that night. Leave with the work done and the trick of it learned.

Exercise two · the second week

The teach-back.

Whoever finished a task last week now runs the session for someone new — on the newcomer's task, not their own. The four habits fit on an index card; hand them the card at the end.

Exercise three · every council

The honest-requirements drill.

Before any task is spoken, say it plainly to a neighbor first: what is actually needed, and by when. If the neighbor cannot repeat it back, the requirement is not honest yet. Then give it to the apprentice.

VII · THE CODAWhy this club exists

Spruce Grove — the apprentice this club teaches — is grown from the same open-source root as Code Puppy, replanted around values of its own: together we are better, always. The story above is why the Leather Apron Club holds its councils: because the method is not an enterprise secret. It is a community craft — older than any of this. Franklin's circle called it mutual improvement. The shops called it apprenticeship. We call it Friday.

Bring one honest question. Bring one task you would love to never do again. The tools will meet you where you already stand — that is the entire discovery, and it is reproducible in any room with a table and enough chairs.
